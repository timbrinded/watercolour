# Pastorals: winter fields, ploughed earth, frost and bare trees

Read this before painting open farmland from an idea: a ploughed field, furrows on a slope, a winter sky with a warm glow under the blue, clinging frost, a hedgerow, a bare tree, a gate or an implement. Every sheet behind it is a winter field in low sun. Green pasture, standing crops, summer foliage, livestock and buildings were not tried; for those, only the notes on layout and sky apply.

Open farmland fails in two opposite ways. It comes out as a tidy diagram: a level horizon, furrows fanning from one point, one tree in a third, ridges ruled like corduroy. "Layout" and the first table under "Ploughed ground" deal with that. Or it comes out as a few pale washes that leave the earth weightless. "Light, value and paints" and the strengths under "Ploughed ground" and "Frost" deal with that.

It was worked out in two rounds. The first was four winter-field sheets (768 by 544 to 960 by 640, cold press and rough, harness 0.10.0) with scrap studies and measurements of five agricultural paintings (Pissarro, Millet, Van Gogh, Peter De Wint, James Thomas Watts). The second was a transfer test: a painter working from the first note alone painted two sheets of 960 by 640 on 0.12.0, and a reviewer shown only the picture and its brief called the result a first lay-in ("lines drawn on sand", "nothing says cold"). Its faults were then measured on a deposit chart and a furrow study on 0.13.0 (cold press, paper seed 3, 640 by 400). The strengths given with values were measured on that chart, on the furrow study or on the two transfer sheets. Values are the value view, where bare paper reads 0.96.

## What is settled and what is not

| Passage | Method | State |
|---|---|---|
| A warm glow under a blue sky | A pale glow, fixed, and the blue ended above it | Settled on two sheets |
| Clouds lifted from the sky | `blot`, then every rim softened | Partly: still read as cut-outs |
| Ploughed ground | A wash at the lit value, then each ridge as a shadow flank and a trench | Partly: reads as a ploughed field, and also as corduroy |
| Clods | Dry-brush marks | Measured: texture only, not a dark |
| Rime | Stippled covering white over dark soil | Measured |
| Frost hollows kept as paper | | Not reached |
| A hedge as a mass | | Not reached |
| Twigs | Rigger at 3 to 4, or the pen | Measured |

Where a passage is not reached, the section says what was tried and how it read, so that the next sheet does not repeat it.

## Light, value and paints

- **Decide the hour and the weather first, then hold both ends of the value range.** A bright morning sky (a mean of 0.68 to 0.76) needs dark ground under it. The transfer sheet held its sky at 0.76, but its ground ran from 0.60 to 0.84 and the darkest hundredth of the whole sheet was 0.27, and it read as a dry late-summer afternoon on sand. The furrow study, which read as damp soil, had a mean of 0.47 with its trenches at 0.24.
- **A cast shadow on the ground is darker than the lit ground beside it.** On the transfer sheet a shadow wash of French ultramarine and burnt umber at 0.68 dried at about 0.77 beside sunlit soil at 0.60, and read as a stain. Lay the lit ground everywhere first, `dry`, and glaze the shadow over it.
- **Paper.** `rough` breaks dry-brush soil into craggy clods but mottles an open sky. For clear air, or fine drawing in twigs and spokes, use `cold_press` at about 960 by 640.
- **Five or six paints.** `cobalt_blue` for the sky, `french_ultramarine` for shadow, `yellow_ochre`, `burnt_sienna` and `burnt_umber` for earth, `cerulean_blue` if the frost is to be tinted, and `opaque_white` for rime. The same shadow blue in the far ridge and the foreground soil ties the sheet together.

## Layout

- **Draw the layout as a diagram before any paint.** Plot the crest, the furrow lines and the cast shadows from their coordinates and look at the picture. The transfer test's first layout put the point the furrows converge on inside the sheet, just above the crest beside the tree, and the diagram showed a starburst at once. Moved a tenth of the sheet's width beyond the right edge and a quarter of its height above the top, the furrows swept obliquely as a field.
- **Let the ground slope and hide what lies beyond.** A crest that rises across the sheet lets the belly of the field mask the valley and puts the viewer close to the soil. A distant ridge showing behind the low shoulder alone gives depth.
- **Leave one side of the furrows quiet.** A fallow margin or headland beside the ploughland keeps the furrows from filling the sheet edge to edge.
- **Cast shadows away from the brightest part of the sky as it dried,** not from where it was planned.
- **Cropping is a choice, not a rule.** A boundary tree cut by the top and side edges works, and so does an implement on one side against a hedge on the other. The transfer painter, given those as advice, reproduced the first round's arrangement; choose for the brief.

## The sky

`SKILL.md` has the general method for a sky and for lifting clouds. What open land adds:

1. **Lay the glow pale, into a bed of clear water over the whole sky.** Its upper edge then vanishes. `yellow_ochre` 0.36 with `burnt_sienna` 0.13, from a mop of size 26 at fullness 0.65, laid in one stroke along the foot of the sky, dried as pale apricot at 0.85. At 1.1 it dried as a mustard band at 0.76.
2. **`dry`, then `fix` the sky.** The fix stops the earths lifting into the next wash. It does not stop blue laid over ochre from reading green.
3. **End the blue above the glow.** Rows of a mop of size 40: `cobalt_blue` 0.85 at the top, about 0.6 below it, 0.2 to 0.4 in the third row, nothing stronger than 0.14 after that, and clear water in every row that lies over the glow, all in one wet pass. Cobalt at 0.25 laid over the stronger glow dried olive (179, 185, 167); with clear rows over the paler glow the same place dried neutral (233, 236, 232).
4. **Carry the clear water to the land.** A bed that stops part-way down dries as a tide line across open air. Neither sheet had one.
5. **Clouds are only partly solved.** Softening the upper rim alone left a hard blue ring under every cloud. Softening every rim, upper and lower, with a clean mop of size 12 drained to 0.52, removed most of the ring, and the reviewer still read the clouds as cut-outs with scalloped tops: a blot made of overlapping circles keeps the circles. A sky with no lifted clouds is the safer choice. Dry strokes added to a dried sky read as flat ovals, so leave it.

## Ploughed ground

What reads wrong:

| Shortcut | How it reads |
|---|---|
| Furrows converging on a point inside the sheet | A bicycle wheel or sunburst |
| Wide horizontal bars stacked down the hill | Sedimentary stripes |
| Equal clod strokes at fixed intervals | A brick wall |
| Humped dark strokes floating on smooth clay | Molehills |
| Thin dark lines of one weight over a pale wash | Lines drawn on sand |

**Weight is value, and the usual first-wash strengths are too pale for soil.** A clay of `yellow_ochre`, `burnt_sienna` and `burnt_umber` in the proportions 2.2 : 2.4 : 1, laid as two rows of a mop of size 22:

| Strength | 0.5 | 0.8 | 1.2 | 1.8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dries at | 0.78 | 0.69 | 0.60 | 0.44 |

**A ridge needs a lit flank, a shadow flank and a trench.** The furrow study built each ridge that way in 194 strokes and measured, over its foreground, a mean of 0.47, a lightest tenth (the lit flanks) of 0.64, a darkest tenth (the shadow flanks) of 0.33 and a darkest hundredth (the trenches) of 0.24. A reviewer shown it beside the transfer sheet's ground, and told nothing about either, called the transfer sheet's ground a sandy track with drill lines and the study a ploughed field running up to a skyline, the better of the two by "somewhat": it is still a diagram of one. The method so far:

1. **The whole field at the lit flank's value.** The clay above at 0.6 beside the crest, rising to 1.0 at the foot, in rows of a mop spaced within 1.4 times its `size`. `dry`.
2. **The shadow flank of each ridge as a band,** on the side away from the light. A `round` whose mark is a little under half the distance between furrows at that depth (a `size` of about that distance divided by 4.3, so 4 beside the crest and 10 at the foot where the furrows are 44 pixels apart), in three overlapping runs down the slope, the pressure wandering between 0.6 and 1.0. `burnt_umber` 0.42, `burnt_sienna` 0.30 and `french_ultramarine` 0.28 for each unit of strength, at 0.85 beside the crest and 1.1 at the foot.
3. **The trench at the foot of that band, while the band is wet.** A `round` of 1.3 beside the crest to 3 at the foot, `burnt_umber`, `french_ultramarine` and `burnt_sienna` in the proportions 5 : 3 : 2, at 1.4 rising to 1.9. It dries soft into the band and crisp against the lit flank of the next ridge, which is dry. `dry`.
4. **Vary the furrows.** The study shifted each by up to an eighth of the spacing, and that was not enough: bands of one tone with ruled edges, as sharp beside the crest as at the foot, read as corduroy or a striped awning.

What the reviewer asked for next has not been tried: break the edge of each band into lumps in the nearest third of the field, let the contrast fall away toward the crest, and keep the trench visible as a third, darker tone along the same side of every band (laid into the wet band it merged with it; laid after a `dry` it would stay distinct).

**Clods are texture, not darks.** A `bright` of size 6 laid over at 60 degrees at fullness 0.55 and strength 2.0 to 2.8 darkened a wash of 0.60 to 0.78 by 0.08 to 0.12 on average (the darkest tenth of the mark by 0.15 to 0.21), and a wash of 0.44 by 0.04 to 0.05. At 1.2 the mark was a little paler, and at 3.6 paler still (0.04 to 0.06), because paste skips. Fullness 0.4 halved it. Scatter them in uneven clusters on the lit flanks for roughness, larger toward the viewer, and take the darks from trench strokes: a `round` of size 4 at 2.0 dried at 0.38 over a wash of 0.78 and at 0.24 over one of 0.44. Short hooked darks added along the bands of the furrow study read as stitches, not as clods.

**Incident.** A pair of broken, curving cart ruts, a few pale flints (masked as dots of radius 1.5 or more before the wash, each given a dark crescent beneath once dry) and sparse tufts of stubble on the headland make a field look worked. Let scale carry the distance: thin, cool and close beside the crest, broad, warm and rough at the viewer's knees.

## Frost

- **Rime is stippled covering white, and it shows only over dark soil.** A `stippler` of size 10 with `opaque_white` 4.5 and `cerulean_blue` 0.1, one dab at pressure 1.0 held for 0.8 seconds, leaves broken bars about 16 pixels across:

  | Ground under it | One coat, mean | One coat, lightest tenth | Two coats, mean |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | 0.44 | 0.59 | 0.72 | 0.64 |
  | 0.60 | 0.64 | 0.75 | 0.76 |
  | 0.69 | 0.72 | 0.80 | 0.79 |
  | 0.78 | 0.80 | 0.85 | 0.83 |

  Over a ground of 0.60 or lighter one coat moves the value by 0.04 or less and cannot be seen; on the transfer sheet such dabs read as pebbles. Pressure and dwell are what lay it: on the scrap sheet, dabs at pressure 0.4 to 0.6 held for 0.3 seconds left nothing that could be seen, while on the chart a brush at fullness 0.6 laid as much as a full one. `dry` between coats, and keep the patches broken with warm soil left between them, or the rime reads as snow. `body-colour.md` has the values for other brushes; on 0.12.0 and later white pastel is a stronger late light (`dry-media.md`).
- **Hard rims take masking fluid; soil does not.** A line of fluid one to two pixels wide under a dark earth wash dries as a white wire that no later glaze hides. Keep it for the top of a post or rail, a glint on steel, or short broken dashes along a beam. An unbroken masked line along every bar of a gate was legible, but the reviewer read it as an emboss: break it.
- **Frost hollows kept as paper are not reached.** The first round shaded the lower trough of each bare-paper pocket and bit dry-brush clods across its edges. In the transfer test eight pockets, masked as tapered blades along the furrows and treated the same way, read as feathers or shards of glass lying on the field. Three things are known. A tint needs to reach about 0.4 in strength to show on paper (`cerulean_blue` 0.1 with `french_ultramarine` 0.06 dried at 0.92). `cerulean_blue` with `cobalt_blue` alone dries turquoise and reads as water, so keep ultramarine in it (`cerulean_blue` 0.22, `french_ultramarine` 0.14, `cobalt_blue` 0.08 dried a blue-grey at 0.88). And pockets of one shape, one size and one direction read as objects. Until a sheet solves this, carry the frost with rime over dark soil and with rims.

## Trees, hedges, implements

- **A bare tree.** Stagger the junctions of the main boughs along a gently crooked trunk; do not fan them from one hub. Give the trunk a taper and a flare at its foot: a trunk of one width read as a post. It is one of the darks of the sheet, so lay its shadow side twice; one coat of `burnt_umber` and `french_ultramarine` charged into a wet trunk dried at 0.44. Load long limbs at fullness 0.76 to 0.80 and reload half-way, or they break into beads.
- **Twigs must be stronger than they look in the brush.** A `rigger` of size 1.0, full, draws a line one pixel wide: at strength 1.0 it dries at 0.77, at 2.0 at 0.65, at 3.0 at 0.57 and at 4.0 at 0.46 (`burnt_umber`, `french_ultramarine` and a little `burnt_sienna`). Against a sky of 0.75 a twig under 3 does not show; the transfer sheet's first twigs at 1.95 vanished. For a twig darker than 0.46, draw it with the pen (`pen-ink-and-fixative.md`). Twigs that cross the brightest part of the sky can be a step paler than the rest.
- **A hedge as a mass is not reached.** Laid wet and charged with a dark at 2.9 from a brush at fullness 0.6, it dried as an even brown loaf. Darks then added on the dry mass (a `round` of 5 to 6 at 1.9) dried as separate lozenges, and shoots drawn at 1.6 did not show. A hedge 10 to 32 pixels tall along a crest read as an earth bank. What is known: give it height, draw its shoots at 3 or more, and vary the posts in spacing, height and lean.
- **One observed object anchors the field:** a plough, a harrow, a gate. Preview its silhouette over the latest look with `scripts/preview_pass.py` before laying darks. A gate laid with a `flat` of size 2.8 for each bar on the dried ground read at once as a five-bar gate, and three faults came with it. Drawn square-on over ground that recedes obliquely, it hovers. Cast shadows laid as separate thin lines read as a reflection: lay the shadow as one connected shape, darker than the ground. And a gate with nothing running on from its posts stands alone in the field: give it its hedge or fence.
- **Birds** measure the air between the ground and the sky when they differ in size, wing angle and tone. Six alike read as ticks.

## Strokes and time

The first round's sheets took 237 to 571 strokes and the transfer sheet 800. From 0.13.0 a painting has no stroke budget; on an older version start it with `--stroke-budget 1200`, because the budget cannot be raised later and the last passes (the darks and the repairs) are the ones it cuts. On a sheet of 960 by 640 a pass of 200 strokes takes one to two minutes and each `dry` about three.

## Diagnosing pastoral faults

| What `look` shows | Likely cause | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| The field reads as sand or a beach | Earth wash under strength 1.2, with no shadow flanks | A lit wash of 0.6 to 1.0, shadow flanks at 0.85 to 1.1 over it and trenches at 1.4 to 1.9 |
| Furrows read as drawn lines | Trench strokes alone on a flat wash | A shadow band beside every trench, widening toward the viewer |
| Furrows read as spokes | They converge on a point inside the sheet | Move that point well outside the sheet; check on a diagram |
| Furrows read as corduroy | Bands of one tone with ruled edges, the same from crest to foot | Not solved: lumpy edges near the viewer, less contrast beside the crest, a distinct trench |
| The ground reads as stacked bands or a brick wall | Wide horizontal bars, or clods at equal intervals | Bands that follow the furrows; clods in uneven clusters |
| A cast shadow reads as a stain | It dried lighter than the lit ground | Glaze it over the dried lit wash |
| Rime cannot be seen, or reads as pebbles | The ground under it is lighter than about 0.5, or the dab was light and brief | Darker soil; pressure 1.0 held for 0.8 seconds; a second coat |
| White wires across the soil | Long fine lines of masking fluid | Keep fluid for rims |
| Frost pockets read as feathers, glass or puddles | Paper reserves of one shape with clean edges | Not solved: use rime and rims |
| An olive band in the sky | Blue laid over the glow | Clear water in every row over the glow |
| A blue ring under a cloud | Only its upper rim was softened | Soften every rim, or leave clouds out |
| Twigs cannot be seen | A rigger under strength 3 | 3 to 4, or the pen |
| The hedge reads as a loaf or a bank | An even wet mass with no height | Not solved: height, shoots at 3 or more, uneven posts |
| The gate hovers | Thin separate shadow lines; nothing continuing from its posts | One connected cast shadow; a boundary |
| Sky reads coarse | A clear sky on `rough` paper | `cold_press` |
