# Pastorals: fields, skies, earth and winter light

Read this before painting open countryside, ploughed fields, rolling hills, hedgerows, frost, farm implements or rural skies from an idea. Open farmland invites two opposite traps: building a tidy diagram (a level horizon, converging spokes, one polite tree in a third) or laying the ground in a few broad strokes that leave the earth empty at close range. A pastoral reads as a place when its land has weight, its light belongs to a particular hour and weather, and its ground rewards looking into.

This note was worked out across four winter-field sheets (768 by 544 to 960 by 640, cold press and rough, harness 0.10.0) and scrap studies, alongside tonal and compositional measurements of five historical agricultural paintings (Pissarro, Millet, Van Gogh, Peter De Wint and James Thomas Watts). The notes on late covering lights and dry media cross-reference 0.11.0 and 0.12.0; take the observations as painterly guidance rather than fixed recipes, and test mixes on the scrap sheet before committing to the full ground.

## Mood, light and the choice of paper

Decide the weather and the hour before choosing either the support or the value range.

- **Separate boldness from gloom.** Committing to rich darks in the foreground does not mean giving up a bright morning sky. An overcast, low-key sheet carries gravity and silence, yet if the brief asks for crisp morning air, a dark brooding sky will make the painting feel bleak regardless of how cleanly it is laid. You can hold a bracing, luminous sky (around 0.68 to 0.76 in mean value, with generous bare-paper highlights) while still anchoring the foreground with deep darks inside clod crevices, weathered timber, iron and bark.
- **Choose the support for the sky as well as the earth.** `rough` paper brings out coarse mineral granulation and breaks a laid-over dry brush into bold, craggy soil clods with broad white pits, but it mottles any open sky into coarse grain. When the weather asks for clear, cold air — or when the foreground needs fine drawing in twigs, spokes, flint stones and stubble — `cold_press` at a generous size (about 960 by 640) keeps the sky clean while giving small brushes room to work. Keep `rough` for weather whose sky welcomes heavy mottling and texture.
- **Keep the paint box narrow so land and sky belong together.** Five or six paints are enough for a winter pastoral: a smooth sky blue (`cobalt_blue`), an icy highlight or frost blue (`cerulean_blue`) if the air is cold, a granulating shadow blue (`french_ultramarine`), and two or three earths (`yellow_ochre`, `burnt_sienna`, `burnt_umber`), plus `opaque_white` if late rime glints will be needed. Letting the same shadow blue cool both the distant ridge and the foreground clay ties the whole sheet into one morning.

## Land as topography, not a stage floor

A level horizon running straight from edge to edge with hills stacked along its back tends to read like painted scenery behind a flat floor.

- **Let the ground slope, swell or hide what lies beyond.** Tipping a ploughed ridge gently across the sheet — rising from a low shoulder on one side to a higher crest on the other — lets the convex belly of the field mask the valley behind it and puts the viewer down close to the soil. A subtle distant ridge peeking behind only the low shoulder gives miles of depth without flattening the foreground.
- **Take your shadows from the light on the sheet, not from memory.** Once the sky has dried, look at where the brightest opening actually landed and cast every shadow away from it. A low sun breaking behind a tree or fence sends cool shadows radiating forward toward the viewer across the slope; low raking light from one side grazes the lit flank of every clod, post and trunk and stretches crisp shadows obliquely across the furrows.
- **Risk asymmetry and cropping.** Placing a lone tree whole inside a third against the brightest patch of sky works cleanly, yet it can feel composed to a formula. Letting a heavy boundary trunk slice straight off the top and side edges of the sheet — or splitting attention across the width between a working implement on one side and a tangled hedgerow on the other — creates quiet tension and makes the landscape feel larger than the frame.

## Skies over open land

- **Warm light under cool blue without greening or tide lines.** When pale morning gold or apricot belongs near the horizon beneath a blue sky, laying the warm glow first into a full-sky bed of clear water lets its upper boundary vanish with no hard edge. Once dry, `fix` on the sky band (kept clear of any foreground masking fluid) prevents warm earth pigments from lifting into the next wash and turning green; on 0.11.0 and later a dried wash already lies beneath a new layer, though fixing still stops soft earths from stirring. Lay the graded blue from the top and carry clear water cleanly all the way down to the horizon in the same wet pass: pigment in a film travels to the film's boundary, so a clear-water feather that stops halfway down the sky dries as a tide line across open air.
- **Tend the edges of lifted clouds.** Lifting pale winter clouds out of a shiny blue wash with `blot` gives airy sunlit puffs, provided their upper rims are softened at once with a clean mop drained to about 0.52. A blot left untended dries with a sharp blue ring that reads as a cut-out patch.
- **Leave a finished sky alone.** Dry corrective strokes added later over a dried sky read as flat ovals. If a quiet opening is meant to glow behind backlit twigs or an implement, let bare paper carry it and stop early.

## Turning soil: furrows that do not read as stripes

Ploughed earth is one of the easiest pastoral passages to make look mechanical. Four shortcuts broke down across the studies:

| Shortcut | How it reads on the sheet |
|---|---|
| Radial lines converging to a point near the middle of the sheet | A bicycle wheel or sunburst rather than a field |
| Wide horizontal mop or `cats_tongue` bars stacked down the hill | Sedimentary stripes rather than cut, overturned earth |
| Equal-width clod strokes stepped at fixed intervals across rows | Vertical columns of dashes — a brick wall |
| Isolated humped dark strokes floating on smooth clay | Little molehills rather than turned slices of soil |

What read as genuinely ploughed earth was built in layers of diminishing size:

1. **One connected earth wash first.** Lay the slope as a continuous wet-in-wet bed of warm sunlit clay and cool shadow washes, keeping overlapping rows within about 1.4 times the brush `size` wherever the ground should close cleanly so unintended horizontal white slits do not slice a quiet slope. Reserve bare paper deliberately only where frost drifts or frozen hollows belong.
2. **Depth through compression of scale.** Whether furrows sweep obliquely toward one side (leaving the opposite edge a quieter fallow margin or headland) or curve broadly across a rising convex hill, let perspective live in scale and value: thin, cool, tightly spaced ridges near the crest, opening steadily down the slope into broader, warmer, rougher waves at the viewer's knees.
3. **Continuous trenches with irregular clods.** A ploughshare cuts an unbroken line, so give each furrow a continuous, gently undulating shadow trench laid in two or three staggered, overlapping runs whose pressure swells where earth overhangs and pinches thin where light enters. Then pack angular, faceted clods — `bright` or `flat` heads turned with varied `twist`, held slightly laid over (`angle` 54 to 68) and semi-dry so the grain breaks them up — in uneven clusters along the sunlit shoulders, with crisp darker shadow pockets tucked on the shaded side of individual clods.
4. **Lived-in rural incident.** A field looks inhabited when small working details interrupt the rhythm of the furrows: a pair of broken, slightly curving cart-wheel ruts pressing across the foreground rows; a scatter of small pale flint or chalk stones sitting just above crisp dark shadow crescents; and sparse, angled tufts of half-buried winter stubble poking out of dark crevices.

## Frost, rime and pale ground texture

Clinging hoarfrost is not a blanket of snow: it reads as frost because cold white and icy blue sit right beside warm, damp bare earth.

- **Keep long thin masking lines out of tilled soil.** A one-to-two-pixel line of masking fluid under a dark earth wash dries as a razor-sharp white needle. Even glazing cool blue over it and crossing it with dark clod strokes leaves rigid white wires embedded in the clay. Save fine masking fluid for **hard structural rims** that naturally hold a sharp line of overnight rime: the flat top of a fence post, an upper timber rail, a glint on a curved steel mouldboard, or short broken dashes along the top edge of a wooden plough beam.
- **Carve frost hollows negatively, then crumble their borders.** Bare-paper pockets left between field washes look like smooth cut-out puddles until two things happen: a pale icy shadow (`cerulean_blue` with a touch of `cobalt_blue` or `french_ultramarine`) shades the lower trough inside the pocket so the hollow drops into the ground, and rough dry-brush clay clods bite unevenly across any straight paper step along its edges.
- **Dust cold clod crests with powdery rime last.** Once the sculpted soil is dry, a badger `stippler` — whose stiff, parted hairs scatter uneven specks over the grain rather than laying a flat band — loaded with `opaque_white` cooled by a trace of `cerulean_blue` or `cobalt_blue` can lay irregular, clod-sized broken patches of silvery rime along the cold shoulders and recessed hollows, in two dried coats where the coldest foreground crests should sparkle. Keeping those patches broken and leaving most of the warm sunlit clay untouched is what makes the rime read as clinging frost rather than snow. On 0.11.0 and later, covering white settles cleanly over darks without greying into them (`body-colour.md`); on 0.12.0, light-pressure white pastel offers a crisp grain-peak frost option in mixed-media work (`dry-media.md`).

## Trees, hedgerows, implements and scale

- **Gnarled winter trees.** Stagger primary branch junctions along a gently crooked trunk instead of fanning every bough from a single hub. On cold press and especially on rough paper, load generously (`water` 0.76 to 0.80) and reload midway down long dark limbs so the brush does not skip into dotted beads. Let warm side-light dry-brush along the sunlit flank of a thick foreground trunk, lighten fine twigs slightly where they cross directly in front of a blinding horizon break, and touch in only a few sparse flecks of dry bronze winter leaves where they add warmth without closing the airy twig lace.
- **Winter hedgerows and boundary posts.** Lay a hedge first as one connected dark mass with warmer sunlit crowns and deeper shadow pockets inside it, then break its smooth silhouette with crossing dark and sunlit thorny shoots, arching bramble canes and weathered leaning fence posts. Vary post spacing, height and lean; regular intervals read as a picket diagram.
- **Working implements and birds.** One carefully observed rural object — a wheeled iron-and-timber plough whose spoked wheels let bright frost show through, a harrow, a weathered field gate — anchors a pastoral far more vividly than another row of generic trees. Preview intricate silhouettes over the latest look with `scripts/preview_pass.py` before laying darks, seat wheels and bases firmly with cast shadows on the soil, and use a small ragged scatter of rooks or crows (varying their size, wing angle and tone between perched, foraging and wheeling birds) to measure the air between the foreground earth and the high sky.

## Diagnosing pastoral faults

| What `look` shows | Likely cause | What helps next pass or next sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Sky looks coarse and porridge-like | Clear sky painted on `rough` paper | Use `cold_press` for clear air; keep `rough` for heavy, mottled weather |
| Crisp winter morning reads as bleak and depressing | Heavy dark overcast sky or too much mid-dark wash in Pass 1 | Hold a luminous sky and reserve darks for clod undercuts, bark, iron and crevices |
| Furrows read as bicycle spokes or a starburst | Vanishing point placed mid-sheet with rays fanning to both lower corners | Sweep furrows obliquely toward one side with a quieter fallow margin, or arch them across a convex rising hill |
| Ground looks like stacked horizontal bands or a brick wall | Broad edge-to-edge mop bars, or clod strokes stepped at equal intervals | Combine continuous pinched-and-swelled trenches with unevenly clustered, angled clods |
| Thin white scratches or wires across dark soil | Long fine masking-fluid strokes laid over ploughed earth | Reserve masking for hard structural frost rims; build soil frost from negative hollows and a cool-tinted badger `stippler` |
| Frost patches read as smooth cut-out puddles | Bare-paper reserves left with clean wash edges and no interior tone | Shade the lower trough with icy blue and bite rough dry-brush clay clods across the straight edges |
| Unintended white slits down a calm frosted slope | Mop rows spaced wider than about 1.4 times `size` | Keep wet row spacing inside 1.4 times `size` where a slope should close cleanly |
| Cut-out ring round a bright cloud | `blot` lifted in a wet sky without softening its rim | Sweep a clean mop drained to about 0.52 lightly along the cloud's upper edge while shiny |
| Thick tree limbs break into dotted beads | Brush running dry on rough or cold-press grain along a long dark stroke | Load at `water` 0.76 to 0.80, move slower on primary boughs and reload before long limbs |
| Winter hedgerow reads as smooth brown loaves | Rounded wash mounds left unbroken | Tuck dark interior pockets inside the hedge and burst its outline with crossing thorny twigs and posts |
